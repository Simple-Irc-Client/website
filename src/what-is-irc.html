a short guide

What is Internet Relay Chat (IRC)?

An open chat protocol from 1988 that is still how a lot of open-source projects talk to each other. Where it came from, how it works, which networks and clients people use today.

Origins

Jarkko Oikarinen wrote IRC in August 1988 at the University of Oulu in Finland, as a replacement for a program called MUT (MultiUser Talk) on the OuluBox BBS. The first server was tolsun.oulu.fi. The first network appeared when Jyrki Kuoppala set up a second server after the university released the code, and by mid-1989 there were about 40 servers worldwide.

The protocol was written down in RFC 1459 (May 1993) and updated in RFC 2812 (2000). Usage peaked around 2004–2005 at roughly 10 million simultaneous users. It fell with the rise of social media but never went away: IRCLab counts around 300,000 users connecting daily across more than 120,000 channels, many of them open-source projects and developer communities.

During the 1991 Soviet coup attempt, IRC was one of the ways news got out while the media were blacked out.

How it works

You run a client, which connects to a server. Servers link together into a network, so people on different servers of the same network can talk. You pick a nickname and join channels; anything you send to a channel goes to everyone in it.

Channels
Group chat rooms, named with a leading #, like #linux or #python.
Private messages
One-to-one conversations between two users.
Nicknames
Unique names, which many networks let you register with their services.
Operators
Channel and server admins who handle access and moderation.
DCC
Direct Client-to-Client connections, used for file transfers.
TLS
Encrypted connections between your client and the server.

IRCv3

The protocol is still being extended by the IRCv3 Working Group, which includes people from InspIRCd, UnrealIRCd, WeeChat, HexChat, Irssi and other projects. Its specifications build on the original RFCs and stay backward compatible, so old clients and servers keep working next to new ones. Some of what it added:

SASL
A standard way to log in to your account while connecting.
Message tags
Metadata attached to messages, used for things like replies and reactions.
Chat history
Server-side history, so you can read what you missed.
Server time
Accurate timestamps on every message.
Account tracking
Clients can see which account a user is logged in as.
Batches
Related messages delivered as a group.
STS
Strict Transport Security: the server tells clients to switch to TLS automatically.

Work continues on account registration, Unicode nicknames and avatars.

Networks

There is no single IRC service. There are many independent networks, each with its own servers, rules and people. The better-known ones:

Libera Chat
The largest network, around 34,000 users and 22,000 channels. Started in May 2021 as Freenode's successor and home to Ubuntu, Fedora, Arch Linux, PostgreSQL and many other projects.
Undernet
Founded in October 1992, now the second largest with around 20,000 users and 5,700 channels, many of them in Latin America and Europe. Runs its own channel registration service, CService.
OFTC (Open and Free Technology Community)
Around 18,000 users and 4,200 channels, focused on free software. Home to Debian, Tor and others.
EFnet
One of the original networks, born from the first big network split in August 1990. About 50,000 users at its 2000 peak. Still runs without nickname or channel services.
HybridIRC
Around 17,000 users. Known for the hybrid IRCd server software.
IRCnet
Formed in July 1996 in "The Great Split". Around 16,000 users and 9,200 channels today, about 70,000 at its 2000 peak. Popular in Europe, no central nickname or channel registration.
DALnet
Forked from Undernet in 1994 and the first network to offer nickname and channel registration. Friendly to newcomers.
QuakeNet
Started in 1997 for Quake players and peaked at 240,000 users in 2004. Still active, mostly gaming and European communities.
Rizon
Communities around anime, gaming and software development.

Clients

There is a client for every platform and taste, graphical or in the terminal. A few that are widely used:

Simple IRC Client
The one this site is about. Desktop app plus a web version, end-to-end encrypted private messages, AGPL-3.0. Windows, Linux, macOS, web.
HexChat
Graphical client based on XChat, with tabs, scripting and lots of plugins. No longer developed: the project was archived in 2024. Windows, Linux, macOS.
irssi
Terminal client scripted in Perl, a long-time favourite of sysadmins. Linux, macOS, Windows (WSL).
WeeChat
Fast, extensible terminal client with scripting in Python, Perl, Ruby, Lua and more. Linux, macOS, Windows (WSL).
mIRC
The classic Windows client, first released in 1995, with its own scripting language. Shareware. Windows.
Konversation
Easy-going graphical client for KDE. Linux.
Textual
Native macOS client with scripting, inline media and iCloud sync. macOS.
The Lounge
Self-hosted web client that stays connected on your server while you're away. Web.

Why people still use it

Nobody owns IRC. Anyone can run a server or write a client, and most networks are run by volunteers. You don't need an account, there are no ads and nothing is tracking you. The protocol is plain text, so it runs on almost anything over almost any connection, and writing a bot takes an afternoon (see write your own IRC client). After more than thirty years, the servers and clients are about as well tested as software gets.
