How the end-to-end encryption works

Simple IRC Client can encrypt direct messages so only you and the person you're talking to can read them. This page walks through SIC-E2EE, the protocol behind it, from the handshake to what ends up on the wire.

The handshake

Before the first encrypted message, the two clients run a short handshake. Your client makes a fresh one-time key pair and sends its public half, together with your long-term identity key, in an OFFER. The other client answers the same way with an ACCEPT.

Neither message contains the session key. Each side computes it on its own with a triple Diffie-Hellman (3DH) over the identity and ephemeral keys, then runs the result through HKDF-SHA256 with a hash of the handshake transcript as salt. The server, and anyone relaying your connection, sees two opaque CTCP lines and has nothing to derive the key from.

Because the session key depends on ephemeral keys made fresh for that conversation, a copy of your long-term identity key can't decrypt messages from a past session. That is forward secrecy.

The cipher

Once both sides hold the same session key, every message goes through the same six steps before it is sent:

  1. Plaintext: the message you typed.
  2. HKDF-SHA256: derive the session key.
  3. AES-256-GCM: encrypt it with a fresh 12-byte nonce.
  4. Base64: encode, split into chunks of at most 320 characters.
  5. CTCP SICE: wrap each chunk as frame n/total, up to 16 frames.
  6. PRIVMSG: send it like any other IRC message.

AES-256-GCM is an authenticated cipher. Along with the ciphertext it produces a tag that proves nothing was changed in transit, and every frame gets its own nonce. Long messages are split into frames and put back together on the other side, with a 30-second window for frames that arrive out of order.

A client that doesn't know SIC-E2EE sees an unknown CTCP request and ignores it, which is why encrypted and unencrypted users can share a network without anything breaking. /me actions go through the same pipeline. An encrypted conversation never falls back to plain text on its own.

Where the keys live

Each IRC network gets its own identity key pair, generated once and stored in IndexedDB as a non-extractable WebCrypto CryptoKey. Once a key is marked non-extractable, no JavaScript on the page can read the private key as raw bytes, including a malicious script injected through an XSS bug. Signing and key derivation happen inside the browser's crypto engine. The key exists, but nothing in the client can print it, upload it, or ever needs to.

Using a separate identity per network also means that someone who knows you on one network can't match your fingerprint to the same nickname on another.

Checking who you're talking to

Encryption alone doesn't tell you whose key you got. In principle, someone who can intercept the first handshake could offer their own key instead of your peer's. The lock icon in the conversation header always shows where you stand:

verified
Encrypted, and you've confirmed the fingerprint.
unverified
Encrypted, but you haven't compared fingerprints yet. This is the normal starting state, not a warning.
mismatch
Your peer's key changed unexpectedly.
off
Plain text. The server can read it.

Messages are protected from the moment either of you starts an encrypted conversation; verification doesn't hold anything up. It is there for when you want to rule out interception during that first handshake. The client spells each side's fingerprint in the NATO phonetic alphabet, one word per hex digit (Zero One Two Three…), so you can read it to each other over something you already trust: a phone call, an existing verified conversation, or in person.

If a verified peer's key changes later, or a conversation that used to be encrypted falls back to plain text, you get a warning before your next message goes out.

What it protects, and what it doesn't

Protected

  • The content of direct messages and /me actions between two Simple IRC Client users
  • Past sessions, through forward secrecy
  • Message integrity, through the AES-GCM authentication tag
  • Your messages from the IRC server and the network operators
  • Your messages from passive eavesdroppers on the network path

Not protected

  • Channels. Everyone in a channel can read it, by design.
  • Metadata: that you're talking to someone, when, and how often.
  • A compromised device. If someone can read keys off your machine, encryption can't help.
  • Peers using a client without SIC-E2EE. The conversation just won't offer encryption.
  • Denial of service. Handshake flooding is rate-limited to one OFFER per peer per second, not prevented.

Don't take this page's word for it: the protocol and its implementation are on GitHub. To try it, open the web chat and start a private conversation with another Simple IRC Client user.
